MEERA PILLAI (SKINSTINCT) - VOICE & STYLE ANALYSIS
Based on 15 pieces: 4 LinkedIn posts + 11 email newsletters
======================================================================

1. HOW SHE OPENS
----------------------------------------------------------------------
She never warms up. The first line is always one of three things:

- A concrete fact that undercuts something the reader believes.
  "The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the ingredient listed
  at 5% or 10%... That number is almost certainly meaningless."
- A scene with a date and place.
  "In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting..." and
  "Last September I was at a trade fair in Mumbai."
- A statement of intent, often admitting she's hesitant.
  "I want to start with something direct." / "I've been avoiding writing
  this one." / "I almost didn't write this email."

The LinkedIn posts open with evidence or a scene. The newsletters open
with "Hi," and then say what the email is about, often admitting some
reluctance. She never uses a question hook, a bold one-liner or
"Let's talk about...".


2. SENTENCE LENGTH AND RHYTHM
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- Sentences average about 17 words, with a median of 14. Roughly 16%
  are very short (six words or fewer) and about 14% run past 30 words.
- Her signature rhythm is a long explanation followed by a short, flat
  punchline.
  "Most serums don't list their pH on the label. This is legal. It is
  also not helpful."
  "The actives were intact. The formulation was working as designed. It
  was designed for the wrong context."
- She uses pairs of contrasting sentences.
  "Niacin is not inert. It causes vasodilation."
  "The ingredient is present. The formulation conditions for efficacy
  are not."
- She builds lists of three: three conditions, three things the label
  doesn't tell you, three requirements.
- She uses no exclamation marks at all, and there are only two question
  marks in the whole set. Both are quoted or reflective, never aimed at
  the reader.
- Her paragraphs are medium length and each one covers a single idea.
  She doesn't use one-line paragraphs for effect the way LinkedIn
  writers usually do.


3. USE OF DATA AND EVIDENCE
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- She's very precise with numbers: pH 3.2 against 3.8, 23% of returns,
  71% of them from humid cities, falling to 8%, a 67% repeat rate
  against an industry 20-40%, 2mg/cm2, 26 EU allergens.
- She uses her own data before outside data: her return figures, CoA
  sampling, 14 months of Vitamin C trials. Outside studies appear only
  rarely, and she names them (University of Liverpool, 2022).
- She uses ranges and hedges when the evidence calls for it:
  "somewhere between 1-3%", "effect sizes that range from modest to
  meaningful." She tells you how strong the evidence is: "solid and
  replicable", "thinner", "essentially absent."
- She shows the mechanism behind a fact, so the reader can check her
  reasoning, rather than just stating the fact.
- She uses documents as proof: CoA, INCI position, temperature logs,
  stability protocols.


4. WHAT SHE NEVER SAYS
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- She never hypes. You won't find "game-changer", "obsessed",
  "holy grail", "glow", "skin-loving" (she puts that one in scare
  quotes) or "clean" without questioning it.
- She never asks readers to act: no "shop now", no "link in bio", no
  discount codes. She repeatedly tells readers she isn't selling
  ("I'm not selling a sunscreen").
- She doesn't use emojis, hashtags, exclamation marks or rhetorical
  questions.
- She doesn't call out competitors by name. She criticises the system,
  not people ("I'm not saying the people in that room were incompetent").
- She doesn't claim authority she doesn't have: "I'm not a
  dermatologist. I don't have a medical degree."
- She doesn't make absolute claims or tell scare stories. Even parabens
  get a fair hearing.
- She doesn't lecture about feelings or self-care, and she never
  mentions how skin looks: no beauty ideals, no "confidence."


5. HOW PERSONAL SHE GETS
----------------------------------------------------------------------
She's personal about her work and her decisions, but reveals almost
nothing about her private life.

- She does share her career history, the meeting that made her leave,
  her mistakes ("embarrassingly long", "should have been right the
  first time"), her frustration and what she's proud of
  ("But I'll take it").
- She doesn't share family, her body or her own skin, her daily
  routine, her feelings about being a founder, or struggle stories. Her
  emotions stay small and specific: "discomfort", "frustrating",
  "I've been sitting with."
- She describes her own origin story as "not a particularly inspiring
  story". She plays her story down on purpose.


6. HER OWN RECURRING MOVES
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- She pre-empts the wrong reading. "I'm not saying X. I'm saying Y."
  It appears in almost every piece, 11 times as "I'm not...".
- She admits her weaknesses before anyone can point them out: no
  Vitamin C product yet, early-stage testing, the humidity mistake.
- She tells readers to question any brand, including hers. "Not from us
  specifically - from any brand." Most pieces end by telling readers to
  ask a brand for documentation.
- She has a stock closing line: "that's useful information" or "that
  tells you something useful." It appears three times word for word,
  and in other forms elsewhere.
- She uses "I" for her judgements and "we" for what the company does.
  Roughly 90 uses of "I" against 25 of "we", so she writes as one
  accountable person.
- She speaks directly to the reader as "you" (66 times), as an equal
  who can evaluate the evidence. She doesn't talk down to them.


7. STRUCTURE
----------------------------------------------------------------------
  1. A hook: a fact or a scene.
  2. The mechanism: why it happens.
  3. Nuance: "I'm not saying...".
  4. What Skinstinct does, stated plainly, often including a
     shortcoming.
  5. What the reader should do, usually "ask for documentation."
  6. For newsletters, the sign-off is just "Meera". No "xoxo", no
     "Best."


8. WORD CHOICE AND REGISTER
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- Her tone is precise, like a scientist writing a review, but she keeps
  it readable.
- She uses technical terms (vasodilation, stratum corneum,
  transepidermal water loss) and explains each one in plain words
  straight after.
- Her spelling is British and Indian: oxidise, colour, sensitisation,
  oestrogenic.
- Words she relies on: "actually", "specifically", "the gap",
  "documentation", "meaningful", "honest/honestly", "properly".
- She sets examples in Indian cities (Mumbai in July, Chennai in
  August), which grounds her writing locally without making it feel
  folksy.


9. TONE
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- She's calm and a little dry. "She didn't, which is a reasonable
  outcome."
- She's openly sceptical of the industry but not cynical, and she gives
  credit where it's due. Clean beauty "gets some things right."
- Her writing feels trustworthy because she accepts looking worse for
  the sake of accuracy: "It is not the most flattering version of the
  information."


10. WATCH-OUTS IF YOU'RE WRITING AS HER
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- She hedges before and after a point. Don't copy the "I'm not saying"
  move more than once per piece, or it turns into a tic. She already
  comes close.
- She uses "honest" and "genuinely" often, 8 and 6 times. Use them
  sparingly or they stop sounding sincere.
- Invented numbers would sink it. Any figure you add has to be real and
  sourced.
